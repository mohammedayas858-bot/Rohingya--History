Who are the Rohingya?

The Rohingya are a mostly Muslim ethnic group whose home is in Rakhine State, in the west of majority-Buddhist Myanmar, along the border with Bangladesh. They trace their history to the Kingdom of Arakan, and many historians and anthropologists date a Muslim presence there back to Arab traders in roughly the eighth century.

Before 2017, about one million Rohingya lived in Myanmar, mostly in the northern townships of Maungdaw and Buthidaung.

Their history is politically contested. The Myanmar government does not use the name “Rohingya” and treats many of them as recent migrants from Bengal. Rohingya and many scholars hold that Muslims have lived in Arakan for centuries, including before British rule.

Timeline

  • c. 8th century onwardMuslim traders and settlers reach the Arakan coast. Arakan becomes a trading kingdom linked to Bengal.
  • 1784King Bodawpaya of Burma invades and conquers the Kingdom of Arakan.
  • 1824–1886Britain fights three wars in Burma and takes control. Under British rule there was no law preventing migration from Bengal into Rakhine.
  • 1948Burma gains independence. A citizenship act is passed, and the Rohingya are initially recognised as citizens.
  • 1962General Ne Win seizes power in a military coup. Rakhine Muslims are increasingly shut out of civic life.
  • 1978A military operation drives more than 200,000 Rohingya into Bangladesh.
  • 1982A new citizenship law recognises 135 ethnic groups and leaves out the Rohingya. It requires proof of residence before 1824, and most Rohingya become stateless.
  • 1991–1992Renewed operations send around 250,000 Rohingya to Bangladesh.
  • 2012Violence in Rakhine State displaces many Rohingya and deepens segregation.
  • 2015“Race and religion” laws are passed, further restricting minority rights.
  • October 2016A military crackdown follows attacks on border posts, and about 87,000 people flee to Bangladesh.
  • 25 August 2017After attacks by the militant group ARSA on security posts, the Myanmar military launches “clearance operations”. Over 700,000 Rohingya flee to Bangladesh within months. Many Rohingya mark the date as Genocide Remembrance Day.

Today

1M+Rohingya refugees sheltering in Bangladesh

1982Year the citizenship law left most stateless

Most Rohingya refugees live in crowded camps around Cox’s Bazar in Bangladesh. Those who remain in Myanmar face restrictions on movement, education, healthcare and work. Refugees continue to call for safe, voluntary and dignified return, with citizenship and rights.

Life in the camps

Cox’s Bazar in Bangladesh is the world’s largest refugee settlement, home to more than one million Rohingya. Families live in crowded shelters on dusty hills and in open valleys, and depend largely on aid.

  • Danger from weather. Monsoon rains bring floods and landslides. In July 2026 a landslide reportedly killed seven students and a teacher at a school in the camps.
  • Risks for children. Parents say they fear malnutrition, early marriage, child labor, trafficking, abduction and forced recruitment into armed groups linked to Myanmar’s civil war.
  • Women and girls. Rohingya women in the camps face gender-based violence, limited health care, little privacy and language barriers, according to Refugees International.
  • Community support. UN Women runs women’s centers in the camps, and Rohingya-led groups help widows, orphans and survivors of violence.

Students and education

Education is the hope most Rohingya parents hold on to. Children in the camps still say they want to become pilots, doctors or engineers.

6,500+learning centers run by UNICEF and partners by 2024

300,000children reached by those centers, at most

  • Lessons in the camps are informal, based on a Learning Competency Framework, and are often taught in Burmese by volunteer teachers from the refugee community.
  • Classes are crowded. One teacher told AFP he taught 65 students in two shifts, covering Burmese, mathematics, science and life skills.
  • Bangladesh agreed to let humanitarian groups pilot the Myanmar curriculum in the camps, a step welcomed by the UN.
  • Community leaders say there is a severe shortage of teachers, and that camp schooling leaves students ill-prepared for Myanmar’s school system. Youth groups call for higher education for Rohingya students.

Students who won scholarships

Higher education is closed to most Rohingya in Bangladesh and Myanmar, so scholarships abroad have changed some young lives. Maung Sawyeddollah, who won a place at New York University, is profiled below. Others include:

Monjur Rahman and Mohammed Ibrahim

Complementary Pathways scholars, Philippines (2026)

In July 2026 these two young Rohingya refugees from Bangladesh arrived in the Philippines as the first scholars of the 2026–2027 intake of UNHCR’s Complementary Pathways (CPath) Programme, studying with Pathways Pilipinas and Columban College. Mohammed Ibrahim said that before his selection the idea of higher education felt almost imaginary, because the camp offered none. The programme now supports 27 refugee scholars.

Abdul

IIE Odyssey Scholar, Saint Louis University, Philippines

After nearly nine years in a refugee camp, he arrived in the Philippines in 2026 through the CPath Programme. UNHCR describes him as the first Rohingya student to receive the Institute of International Education’s Odyssey Scholarship there.

Shofi Alom

Electrical engineering student, Western Michigan University

He escaped the genocide in Myanmar and came to the United States, where in 2026 he was one of three recipients of a $5,000 scholarship for first-generation students.

  • Canada. A Rohingya student who was refused by one university after another because of limited documents became, with a Duolingo scholarship, the first Rohingya refugee to study in Canada as an international student.
  • United Kingdom. Yaaseir, a pharmacy student at the University of Bath and a Sanctuary Gold Scholar, says he wants to work in healthcare because the Rohingya have too few doctors.
  • Group scholarships. In 2017 Turkey announced university scholarships for 53 Rohingya students, and Kuwait’s Red Crescent has pledged four-year scholarships for 100 Rohingya girls in Bangladesh.

Board examination (EBRR)

On 26 March 2026, Rohingya refugee students in Cox’s Bazar began their first-ever matriculation exams, run by the Examination Board of Rohingya Refugees (EBRR). It is one of the most structured education efforts in the camps since the 2017 exodus.

  • Format. The exams follow a structure similar to Myanmar’s matriculation system, so students can study in a familiar format while displaced.
  • Why it matters. Informal learning centers serve younger children, but formal secondary education has been hard to reach for older students. Organizers hope the exams give students recognized academic results and keep them connected to Myanmar’s system if return becomes possible.
  • Preparation. The board worked for months with camp authorities and security personnel. Question papers and answer scripts were handed to Bangladesh’s Armed Police Battalion under strict security and delivered to exam centers across several camps.
  • Community hope. Community members called the exams a hopeful step for young people facing limits on movement, work and higher education.

This is based on a single news report from March 2026. I could not find the number of students who sat the exams or any results.

Kayaphuri High School

Kayaphuri School, in Kutupalong camp, was the largest private secondary school run by Rohingya refugees in Bangladesh. Community leader Mohib Ullah founded it in 2019, with funding from teachers and refugee families who could afford to contribute. About 600 students studied there, following the same curriculum used inside Myanmar.

2019year the school was founded

~600students enrolled when it closed

  • Why it mattered. UNICEF-supported learning centers serve children aged 4 to 14, so older refugees had few options beyond religious schools and private schools like Kayaphuri.
  • Closure. On 24 March 2022, armed police raided the school while its headmaster, Mohammad Showife, was printing questions for the ninth-grade exams. Police took the computer and printer, and then the classroom seats, and locked the doors. Students said they were in the middle of exams.
  • Official position. Authorities said the school had no permission to operate. The headmaster said its founder had obtained only verbal permission from a government-appointed official.
  • Wider crackdown. Kayaphuri was the last of about 30 refugee-run schools to be shut by police or to close ahead of closures since December 2021, leaving none operating.

Rabeya Akter, an eighth-grade student at the school who dreamed of becoming an engineer, was one of hundreds of students affected. Bangladesh later moved toward allowing the Myanmar curriculum in the camps, as described above.

New Light community high schools

Alongside UNICEF learning centers, Rohingya teachers have built their own community-led schools inside the camps. Two with “New Light” in their names appear in reports: New Light High School in Camp 4 and Mayyu New Light High School in Camp 13.

Mayyu New Light High School, Camp 13

Community-led school

Founded in 2020 by a group of Rohingya community teachers, it began with just 50 students. It now teaches grades 1 to 10, with about 20 community teachers working across 13 learning facilities. One of its co-founders, a teacher who came from Myanmar, serves as acting principal. Because camp authorities do not permit community-led schools, it operates discreetly. It was one of the schools that joined the Examination Board of Rohingya Refugees.

In 2025, 53 community-led schools in the camps, including New Light High School in Camp 4, came together to form the EBRR described above, after months of meetings among their principals. Rohingya teachers say these schools help preserve their culture and identity, and that most teachers work with little or no pay and follow the Myanmar education system.

These details come from Rohingya community news reports and a teacher’s own account, not from official records, so numbers may have changed.

Historical people, scholars, writers and community leaders {#historical-people}

This is a growing directory, not a complete list of every historical person connected to Rohingya or Arakan history. The region has been home to multiple communities, and historical identity labels do not always match modern ethnic categories. For that reason, this section distinguishes people documented as Rohingya from writers, officials and rulers connected to the wider history of Arakan. Inclusion here does not mean that every person listed identified as Rohingya.

Early records and the history of Arakan

Francis Buchanan-Hamilton (1762–1829)

Scottish physician and naturalist whose 1799 account recorded Muslims in Arakan who called themselves “Rooinga.” His report is often discussed in debates about the history of the name. It is one historical record and should be read alongside other documents and scholarship.

Source: Rohingya History — Origins in Arakan.

Narameikhla / Min Saw Mon (reigned from 1430)

The Arakanese ruler associated with the restoration of the Mrauk-U kingdom in 1430 after a period of exile in Bengal. Accounts of this period describe political and cultural links between Arakan and Bengal. His reign belongs to the wider history of Arakan; the available evidence does not establish him as a modern Rohingya figure.

Source: Rohingya History — The Mrauk-U Kingdom.

Writers and poets of the Arakan court

These writers contributed to the region’s literary history. Many wrote in Bengali and worked in a multilingual court society. They should be presented as figures in Arakan’s shared history, not automatically labelled with a modern ethnic identity.

Daulat Qazi (17th century)

Bengali poet associated with the Arakan court. His literary work is part of the history of Bengali-language poetry that flourished in the region.

Alaol / Shah Alaol (c. 1607–1680)

One of the best-known Bengali poets associated with the Mrauk-U court. His works are an important part of medieval Bengali literature and reflect the cultural connections between Arakan and Bengal.

Magan Thakur (17th century)

A Muslim court official and patron of literature associated with the Arakan court and poet Alaol. Sources describe him as an influential figure in the literary world of the period.

Quraishi Magan

A poet named in historical writing about Muslim and Bengali-language literature in Arakan. Details of his life and dates require further source checking before a fuller biography is added.

Abdul Karim Khondkar / Abdul Karim Khandkar (17th–18th century, dates uncertain)

A poet associated with Arakan in some historical accounts. Because spellings and biographical details vary between sources, this website should add a longer biography only after confirming the identity and references.

Mardan and Shah Barid Khan

Names included in accounts of poets associated with the Arakan court and Bengali-language literary tradition. Their biographies and dates need additional corroboration before firm claims are made.

Source for this group: Prof. Dr. Abdul Karim, The Rohingyas: History and Culture and A Short History of Rohingya and Kamas.

Historians and researchers of Rohingya history

Mohammed Ashraf Alam (1948–2021)

Rohingya researcher and community figure born in Kalizabangah village in Maungdaw township. He graduated from Rangoon Arts & Science University in 1970 and later worked with research and publication organisations, including the Bangladesh Institute of Arakan Studies, the Arakan Historical Society and Rohingya Khobor. He was a senior member of the Arakan Rohingya National Organisation (ARNO). His research, including A Short History of Arakan and Rohingyas, remains a reference for people studying Rohingya and Arakan history.

Source: ARNO obituary and biography.

Dr. Abdul Karim

Historian and author of The Rohingyas: History and Culture, a work frequently cited in writing about Rohingya history and the Muslim communities of Arakan. Readers should compare his interpretations with other scholarship and primary records.

Source: The Rohingyas: History and Culture (PDF).

Dr. Mohammad Yunus

Author of A History of Arakan: Past and Present, cited in Rohingya historical writing. A fuller profile should include independently checked biographical details and a review of his publications.

Dr. Mohammad Mohibullah Siddiquee

Scholar associated with the edited volume The Rohingyas of Arakan: History and Heritage. His work is cited by Mohammed Ashraf Alam’s obituary as part of the scholarship to which Alam contributed information and research support.

People and outstanding work

Many Rohingya are lawyers, teachers and community leaders who document what happened and help others. Some well-known examples:

Mohib Ullah

Teacher, community leader and peace activist (1971–2021)

Born in Myanmar and a teacher there, he became known in the camps as “Master Mohib Ullah” and the “Peace Father”. In 2017 he founded the Arakan Rohingya Society for Peace and Human Rights (ARSPH) in Kutupalong camp, which documented the atrocities Rohingya survived and gave refugees a voice. In August 2019 a rally it organized to mark two years since the exodus drew more than 100,000 people. He spoke at the UN Human Rights Council in Geneva in 2019 and was invited to the White House that year. He argued for return to Myanmar with the rights Rohingya had been denied. On 29 September 2021 he was shot dead at his organization’s office in the camps. His family and Bangladeshi police pointed to members of the armed group ARSA, and rights groups called for a full investigation.

Mohammad Nowkhim

Spokesman of the Arakan Rohingya Society for Peace and Human Rights (ARSPH)

He worked alongside Mohib Ullah and served as the spokesman of ARSPH, the refugee-led rights group in Kutupalong camp. When Mohib Ullah was shot dead on 29 September 2021, Nowkhim was the one who told journalists what had happened. He described how Mohib Ullah was talking with other leaders outside the ARSPH office after evening prayers when a gunman shot him, and said Mohib Ullah was taken to a nearby MSF hospital but was already dead. Nowkhim spoke from hiding and said many Rohingya leaders had gone into hiding after the killing. He and other members of the group blamed ARSA for the attack.

Razia Sultana

Lawyer and human rights advocate

Born in 1973 in Maungdaw, Myanmar, and raised in Bangladesh. Since 2014 she has worked with Rohingya women and girls in the camps and documented violence against them. She addressed the UN Security Council in 2018 and received the US International Women of Courage Award in 2019. Her organization has housed widows and orphans and schooled hundreds of children.

Wai Wai Nu

Activist and lawyer

Jailed at 18 as a law student, she served seven years of a seventeen-year sentence in Insein Prison. Freed in 2012, she earned her law degree and founded organizations that support peace-building and give women and youth legal counsel and rights education. She received a Hillary Clinton Award in 2018.

Maung Sawyeddollah

Student and education advocate, New York University

Born in Myanmar, he fled to Bangladesh with his family in 2017 and spent years in the Cox’s Bazar camps. Myanmar had barred Rohingya from higher education, and in Bangladesh there was no legal provision for Rohingya to enroll in universities. Stateless and without a passport, he wrote to institutions and aid groups, ran campaigns and wrote articles calling for Rohingya students’ right to higher study. He applied to well over 100 universities around the world and was rejected by many, including Oxford. Several offered him scholarships, and he went on to New York University, described by the US Embassy in Dhaka as the first Rohingya student there. Bangladesh gave him clearance to travel and the US Embassy issued him a visa without a passport.

Rohel Khan

Rohingya rapper, singer and lyricist

A young artist from the refugee camps of Cox’s Bazar, he describes himself as a Rohingya genocide survivor raising his voice from the world’s largest refugee camp. Besides singing and rapping, he writes lyrics and music and works as an actor, editor and videographer through his own production page, which has close to 200,000 likes on Facebook. His songs, released as music videos on YouTube and shared widely on TikTok, include “Ohh Arakan” about the Rohingya plight, “Deshor Monot” and love songs such as “Sukor Kuna Loi”. His work belongs to a wider revival of Rohingya music in exile, where artists use song to keep their culture alive and tell their story.

Robi Alam

Footballer and captain of Rohingya United

Born in 2000 in a Rohingya refugee camp in Cox’s Bazar, he played with a ball made of plastic bags wrapped in rubber bands, before he had ever watched a match. At ten he was among the few Rohingya resettled in Australia, and he plays in midfield, inspired by Kaká. In 2014 he and other young Rohingya formed a club in Brisbane, and from around 2017 he captained Rohingya United, a team whose name starts conversations about the Rohingya. Off the pitch, he and his teammates welcomed refugees released from detention and joined protests for their freedom. Rohingya teams now play across Australia and in Canada, the US and Malaysia.

Jani Alam

Rohingya folk singer

He keeps the oral traditions of the Rohingya language alive through performance. Forced out of his homeland in Myanmar, he lives in the refugee camps of Cox’s Bazar. His work has been featured in the literary magazine Modern Poetry in Translation, which brings Rohingya folk song to an international audience.

United Council of Rohang (UCR)

The UCR, also reported as the United Council of Rohingya, is a refugee leadership council formed in 2025 after the first elections Rohingya refugees had held in the eight years since the 2017 exodus. More than 3,000 voters took part across 33 camps in Cox’s Bazar.

480–500counsellors chosen to form its congress

28executive members, led by five rotating presidents

Mohammad Sayed Ullah

UCR president

He says the council wants to be the voice of the Rohingya at the negotiating table, since decisions about their future have been made without them. He has urged refugees to prepare to return home and not to forget the violence that drove them out.

What it does. Its leaders speak for refugees on repatriation and camp conditions, and refugees bring complaints about local leaders to its office. In September 2026 UCR leaders called on the international community for a clear, time-bound roadmap for safe, voluntary, dignified and sustainable return to Myanmar. It has also publicly rejected Myanmar government statements that call Rohingya “Bengalis”, insisting on the name Rohingya and on citizenship.

Education. Younger refugees say the council can push for better schooling, which they see as the way to build international support for return.

Not everyone is convinced. Analysts, including the International Crisis Group, have said the elections appeared closely controlled by the authorities and question whether the council can fully represent refugees independently.

Rohingya scholars and historians {#scholars-and-historians}

Rohingya history has been preserved by researchers, teachers, community leaders, writers, and families. This section highlights people whose work helps document Rohingya and Arakan history. Biographical details below are based on the linked sources and should be updated when additional reliable records become available.

Mohammed Ashraf Alam (1948–2021)

Rohingya researcher, historian, and community leader

Mohammed Ashraf Alam was born on 10 January 1948 in Kalizabangah village, Maungdaw Township, Arakan. According to the Arakan Rohingya National Organisation (ARNO), he graduated from Rangoon Arts & Science University in 1970 and dedicated much of his life to Rohingya research and community work. He served in research and publication roles with the Bangladesh Institute of Arakan Studies, the Arakan Historical Society, and Rohingya Khobor, and was a senior member of ARNO.

His research work, A Short History of Arakan and Rohingyas, is cited by ARNO as an important reference for people studying Rohingya history. He died in Chittagong, Bangladesh, on 15 February 2021, aged 73.

Why include him? His life demonstrates the importance of Rohingya researchers who collect historical records, preserve community knowledge, and make it available to future generations.

Rohingya culture and traditions {#culture-and-traditions}

Rohingya culture is carried through everyday practices as well as formal records. Community traditions may include storytelling, proverbs, riddles, songs and ballads, poetry, wedding customs, food, clothing, embroidery, crafts, religious and family celebrations, and traditional games such as boat racing. Practices and vocabulary can differ between villages, families, generations, and places of displacement; no single description represents every Rohingya person.

Oral storytelling is especially important because it passes knowledge and values from elders to younger generations. The Rohingya Cultural Memory Centre, supported by IOM, documents oral traditions and cultural expressions. Its work describes traditions such as proverbs, riddles, folk stories, songs, and the traditional boat-racing activity known as Gadi Kela.

Ideas for future entries:

  • A traditional food or recipe, with the local Rohingya name and the storyteller's permission.
  • A proverb or riddle, with the original wording, translation, meaning, and regional variations if known.
  • A song, poem, wedding custom, craft, or game, including who shared the information and when.
  • Photographs of clothing, tools, crafts, or cultural events, with captions and permission from the photographer and identifiable people.

Source: UNESCO-ICHCAP: Preserving the Oral Traditions and Expressions of the Rohingya Community · Rohingya Cultural Memory Centre

Rohingya language and literature {#language-and-literature}

Rohingya is a living language used by communities in Myanmar, Bangladesh, and the wider diaspora. It is passed down through speech, stories, songs, poetry, proverbs, and family life. Efforts to write and publish Rohingya use more than one writing system, including Hanifi Rohingya and Roman-based forms. Spelling and written conventions may vary, so this website should record which script and spelling system an entry uses rather than presenting one form as the only correct form.

Rohingya literature and language resources can include oral poetry, traditional ballads, folktales, dictionaries, translated materials, and modern writing by Rohingya authors. Where possible, preserve the original text alongside a translation and credit the author, translator, or person who recorded it.

Suggested collection format:

  • Title and genre (poem, song, story, proverb, or language resource)
  • Original Rohingya text and script, if permission is granted
  • Translation and translator's name
  • Author, performer, or knowledge holder (if they wish to be named)
  • Date and place recorded, using only the level of location the contributor agrees to share
  • Copyright or permission status

Sources: Rohingya Cultural Memory Centre · Rohingyatographer: Rohingya resources · Research on Rohingya language policy and writing systems

Maps and historical documents {#maps-and-historical-documents}

Maps, old photographs, books, letters, identity documents, land records, school certificates, newspapers, and government or colonial records can help researchers investigate the history of Arakan and Rohingya communities. Every document should be described carefully: who created it, approximately when, what it shows, where it came from, and what limitations it may have.

A document is evidence of what its creator recorded; it does not automatically prove every claim made about it. Historical records may use older place names, administrative boundaries, or labels that differ from present-day terminology. Official records can also reflect the politics and biases of the period in which they were produced, so compare sources where possible.

Useful public resources:

  • SYNu Rohingya Heritage Archive — a resource hub listing maps, photographs, books, videos, oral-history recordings, and news articles.
  • ARNO: Rohingya History — a historical overview with references to published works and archival records.
  • Mohammed Ashraf Alam: A Short Historical Background of Arakan (PDF) — a historical text attributed to Mohammed Ashraf Alam.

Do not publish private identity cards, family-registration documents, addresses, phone numbers, signatures, or other sensitive details without informed permission. If a document could put someone at risk, describe it without uploading the original or obscure sensitive information before publication.

Oral history and community memories {#oral-history-and-community-memories}

Oral histories can preserve experiences that are missing from official records, including memories of village life, education, language, displacement, family history, and life in refugee camps. They are valuable community testimony, but recollections can differ; record who is speaking and the circumstances of the account instead of treating one person's memory as the experience of every Rohingya family.

Suggested interview questions:

  1. What name would you like us to use, or would you prefer to remain anonymous?
  2. What village or area are you comfortable identifying as your home?
  3. What do you remember about family life, school, language, work, or community traditions?
  4. Which story, object, photograph, or document would you like younger generations to remember?
  5. Is there anything you do not want published or shared online?

Before recording: explain the purpose of the project, where the recording may appear, whether it may be translated or edited, and how the person can contact you about a correction or request. Obtain clear consent before recording or publishing. Consent to an interview is not automatically consent to publish a name, face, voice, photograph, or document. Do not pressure anyone to discuss violence, trauma, legal status, or other sensitive experiences.

Record the interview date, interviewer, language, translator (if any), and permission status. Keep a secure backup and avoid publishing exact locations or identifying details when they could create safety risks.

Rohingya education history {#rohingya-education-history}

Education is an important part of Rohingya community history, including learning in villages in Arakan, the barriers faced by Rohingya children in Myanmar, and community-led education in displacement. In Bangladesh's camps, families, teachers, and community organisations have tried to maintain learning despite restrictions, shortages of resources, and limited pathways to recognised secondary and higher education.

This website already documents recent examples such as Kayaphuri High School, Mayyu New Light High School, community learning centres, scholarship recipients, and the Examination Board of Rohingya Refugees (EBRR). A future education-history collection could add:

  • Names and histories of schools in Arakan, with dates verified through former students, teachers, or records.
  • Former teachers' and students' memories, clearly labelled as oral histories.
  • Changes in teaching languages, curricula, school access, and examination opportunities.
  • Community-run schools and learning projects in Bangladesh and other countries of refuge.
  • Stories of Rohingya students, teachers, and graduates, published only with permission.

When reporting a school's history, distinguish documented dates and enrolment figures from estimates or memories, and add the source for each important claim.

Corrections and contributions {#corrections-and-contributions}

This is a community history project and a work in progress. Readers are welcome to suggest corrections, share additional sources, recommend a person to profile, or offer a story, photograph, map, or document for possible inclusion.

To help review a contribution, please provide:

  • The page or sentence you are referring to.
  • The proposed correction or addition.
  • A source, document, or explanation of how the information is known.
  • The name or credit you want displayed, or a request to remain anonymous.
  • Confirmation that you own the material or have permission for it to be published.

Submissions may be checked before publication. Not every submission can be independently verified, and the site may label information as a personal memory, community account, disputed claim, or unconfirmed detail where appropriate. Please do not send original identity documents or sensitive personal information through public comments.

Privacy and safety: never publish someone's name, face, contact details, exact location, immigration or legal information, or personal testimony without informed consent. Contributors may ask for their name to be omitted. If publishing a story could expose a person to harassment, retaliation, or other harm, do not publish identifying details.

About the creator {#about-the-creator}

Mohammed Ayas is a Rohingya community volunteer and digital-skills freelancer with an interest in education, digital inclusion, humanitarian support, and refugee rights. He completed secondary education and has supported training and educational activities as a Training Support Assistant (TSA) volunteer with BRAC, with support from UNHCR.

He communicates in Rohingya, English, Burmese, and Bengali. Through this website, he hopes to help preserve Rohingya history, highlight community experiences, and make information about Rohingya education and rights more accessible.
